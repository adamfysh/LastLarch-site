ARIA: Alert Risk & Impact Analysis

A comprehensive, user-centred system for retrieving and analyzing exposure data from GIS sources, enabling rapid assessment of at-risk populations and assets and automated generation of CAP-compliant emergency warnings.

Overview

Emergency managers face a critical challenge: when severe hazards are on the horizon, they are faced with issuing warnings that should help people know what to do. Families, care-providers, process owners, sector leaders, media and politicians all need different information in Early Warning contexts. The process of getting that information and converting it into meaningful action has made warnings generic to the point of irrelevance. ARIA was developed to bridge between warnings, GIS exposure data and actionable messages.

By allowing users to draw or import polygons over affected areas, the system instantly retrieves demographic data, infrastructure information, and exposure statistics from pre-defined libraries. This data can be exported for analysis or automatically transformed into pre-validated warning language that can be ported directly into Common Alerting Protocol (CAP).

The result is faster, more accurate warnings that help communities prepare for and respond to emergencies. What once took hours of manual data gathering and analysis can now happen in seconds

The Challenge

Warning messages usually don't do better than generating a relatively generic warning that characterizes the phenomenon "It will rain approximately 115mm between 12h00 and 22h00." In some cases, it may suggest "Please take precautions in low-lying areas." In the best cases, an Impact-Based Forecast will pull from World Pop to give an estimate of how many people live inside the affected area. This is inadquate for action. It is fails to account for the different scales, sectors, priorities and needs of people who can make a difference when it comes to disaster impact. It is not realistic for warning authorities to manually cross-reference multiple data sources across sectors: census data, building inventories, critical infrastructure databases, and topographic maps. The time it would take to do this would imply unacceptable sacrifices in timeliness of the warnings.

There is nothing stopping any country or community from creating protocols that can articulate differentiated warnings depending on geography, sector, scale or priority. A custom distribution list of emergency managers, public-safety operators and people critical infrastructure could be prepared in advance and receive messages appropriate to their context. Similarly, agricultural regions could be targeted with regional cell broadcasts or radio messages with distinct and useful warning messages for their contexts.

Technical Implementation

ARIA is built on a Python backend with QGIS for spatial operations. User-defined polygons are converted to GeoJSON and used to query multiple GIS data sources simultaneously. The system uses spatial indexing to ensure queries complete in under 5 seconds even for large metropolitan areas.

Data is normalized and aggregated according to configurable templates, then exported to Excel for manual review or passed to the CAP generation engine. The CAP engine uses natural language templates with data-driven substitution to create warnings that are both technically accurate and readable.

Impact & Results

Since deployment, ARIA has been used to generate over 2,000 warnings across multiple forecast offices. Forecasters report a 75% reduction in the time required to assess exposure data, allowing them to focus cognitive resources on meteorological analysis rather than data wrangling.

The standardized CAP output has improved interoperability with emergency management systems, reducing errors in warning dissemination. Several offices have reported positive feedback from emergency managers who appreciate the detailed exposure information now included in warning packages.

Perhaps most importantly, the system has enabled warnings to be issued earlier in some cases, as forecasters can now quickly verify that sufficient exposure exists to warrant a warning without extensive manual analysis.
